Worked example · Parameters for the best small bank

The Binding Constraint

Defining the parameters that govern one small bank's credit-card book, and finding the portfolio that earns the most while staying inside every Basel rule. Capital turns out not to be what stops it.

Comenity Bank · RSSD 1391778 Year-end 2025 Call Report $7.8bn assets · 100% credit cards

The project's order of work is rules, then parameters, then use. This is the third step carried out end to end on one bank: every coefficient below is either a number lifted from the Basel Framework with its paragraph cited, or a rate computed from the bank's own filing. Nothing is assumed except where the text says so.

01 — SelectionWhich small bank is the best one

"Best" needs a criterion before it means anything. For a card book the defensible one is risk-adjusted margin — what the portfolio earns after the losses it actually incurs — read alongside return on assets and equity. All three come straight from the filing.

Credit One earns more and cannot be analysed. Its 26.2% return on assets and 92.9% on equity are the best in the population by a wide margin — and it has elected the community bank leverage ratio, so it reports no risk-weighted assets at all. Every capital constraint in this exercise is a ratio to RWA. For Credit One those ratios do not exist, which is a finding about the US framework rather than a gap in the data.

Comenity is the best performer among banks the rules can be applied to. Fourth on risk-adjusted margin but far ahead on returns, at real scale, with a loan book that is 100% credit cards and $37.8bn of committed but undrawn lines. Everything the credit-card rules govern is present in one balance sheet.

03 — ObjectiveProfit, written down

Each asset earns its rate and is funded at the same cost, so contribution is the spread over funding. Undrawn lines earn nothing until they are drawn — they enter only through the constraints they impose.

maximise (0.0955 − 0.0450)·c + (0.0420 − 0.0450)·s + (0.0467 − 0.0450)·h
= 5.05%·c − 0.30%·s + 0.17%·h

The shape of the answer is already visible. A dollar in cards earns 5.05 cents over funding; a dollar in securities loses 0.30 cents; a dollar in cash earns 0.17. A monoline card bank has no attractive alternative to lending, so the question is not what to hold but how much, and what stops it.

This is contribution, not the bank's reported profit. It charges funding against every asset including the equity-funded portion, and uses realised charge-offs rather than the accounting provision. Both make allocations comparable; both are constants with respect to the allocation and so change no decision. Reported pre-tax profit for 2025 was $450.7m.

04 — ConstraintsWhat the rules impose

Capital 0.75(c + 0.10u) + 0.20s ≤ CET1 ÷ 7.0% = $15,066m CRE20.68(1) · CRE20.100 · RBC20.1 · RBC30.2

Leverage c + s + h + 0.10u ≤ Tier 1 ÷ 3% = $35,153m LEV20.7

Liquidity s + h ≥ 0.05u LCR40.64(1), applied as a stress

Funding c + s + h ≤ $9,313m (assets +20%)

Utilisation 10% ≤ c ÷ (c + u) ≤ 25%

The last two are not regulatory. Funding capacity bounds how fast a deposit franchise can grow; without it the problem becomes a statement about capital alone and returns an answer no bank could execute. Comenity's balance sheet shrank from $8.68bn to $7.76bn over the three years to 2025, so permitting +20% is already generous.

Utilisation is a property of the customer base, not a dial. A bank can withdraw lines, raising utilisation; it cannot make customers borrow. Comenity's own range over twelve quarters was 12.1% to 15.2%, so a 25% ceiling is a stretch assumption and is tested in section 06.

Total +$68m, a 20% improvement in contribution. And the larger share comes not from growth but from shrinking something — the undrawn book.

The mechanism is the liquidity constraint. Every dollar of committed line obliges the bank to hold five cents in cash against a drawdown. That cash earns 4.67% while a dollar of card receivable earns 9.55% before funding. Carrying $37.8bn of lines locks up $1.89bn of balance sheet in the lowest-yielding asset it owns. Cutting the line book to $24.3bn releases that capacity into receivables.

What binds, and what does not

Three constraints are active at the optimum: the liquidity cover on committed lines, the funding capacity bound, and the utilisation ceiling. Capital is not among them.

At the optimal book, risk-weighted assets are $7,895m and the 7% requirement calls for $553m of CET1. Comenity holds $1,055m — 1.9 times what it needs. The capital constraint only begins to bite after a 48% loss of CET1.

This inverts the intuition the capital rules invite. For this bank, Basel's capital apparatus is slack; what actually limits profit is the liquidity it must carry against promises it has already made, and the deposits it can raise.

The binding set never changes. Across every combination tested, the same three constraints are active and capital is never one of them. The level of profit moves with the assumptions; the conclusion about what limits the bank does not.

At zero growth the optimum equals today's contribution of $343m, which is the honest reading: with no capacity to expand and utilisation already at its ceiling, there is nothing left to reallocate. The book is entirely credit cards and credit cards are the best asset available. The gains require either new funding or fewer unused lines.

07 — ValidationThe model agrees with management

A portfolio recommendation that no practitioner would recognise is a warning sign. This one is what Comenity has in fact been doing.

Over the twelve quarters to December 2025 the bank withdrew $14.2bn of committed card lines, from $52.0bn to $37.8bn — a 27% reduction — while its utilisation climbed from 12.8% to 15.2%. The optimiser, given only the bank's rates and the rules, independently proposes cutting a further $13.5bn and pushing utilisation higher still.

The model did not have access to that history. It reproduces the direction of travel from the economics alone, which is the strongest evidence available here that the parameterisation is sound.

08 — LimitsWhat this does not do

The transactor split is unobservable, so the 45% risk weight in CRE20.68(2) cannot be claimed and 75% is used throughout. Since capital is slack, this changes no decision here — but it would matter for a thinner-capitalised bank.

Basel and the US rule give the same answer for this bank. Under 12 CFR 217.33(b)(1) the undrawn lines convert at 0% rather than Basel's 10%, and retail carries a flat 100% rather than 75%. Both were run; the allocation is identical, because capital binds under neither. The divergence that matters so much elsewhere in this project is immaterial here, and only because of how much capital this bank carries.

The buckets are coarse. A real allocation would distinguish partner programmes, vintages and credit tiers, each with its own yield and loss curve. Call Report aggregation supports four buckets, not forty.

Rates are held constant. The model assumes the marginal card dollar earns what the average one earns. Growing a book 20% would in practice mean lending further down the credit spectrum, at higher yield and higher loss. That is precisely what the scenario phase should perturb.

What this sets up

The five-step assessment produced a comparable profile; this produces an objective function and a constraint set. A scenario is now something specific: a shock that moves one parameter and re-solves. A funding shock tightens the capacity bound. A drawdown shock raises the 5% cover rate. A migration of the book from regulatory retail to other retail moves the risk weight from 75% to 100%. A loss shock raises the 6.98% charge-off rate until the card margin no longer clears funding.

Each is bounded by something observed in the twelve-quarter panel rather than invented, which is the plausibility test the brief requires.
